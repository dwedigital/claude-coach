# Methodology — The Why Behind Plan Structure

**Purpose:** the evidence base for how plans are built, so prescriptions are traceable and consistent — and so it's clear which principles are settled science and which are useful conventions. A good coach knows the difference. Read this when designing or defending a plan.

**Bottom line up front:**
- **Taper** is the strongest-evidenced lever here — real meta-analytic numbers.
- **~80% easy** is robust; the "polarised vs pyramidal" argument about the other 20% is genuinely unresolved and phase-dependent — don't dogmatise it.
- **Periodisation-per-se, 3:1 loading, supercompensation, the 10% rule, and ACWR** range from weakly-evidenced to heuristic-dressed-as-science. Use them, but don't quote them as settled.
- **CSS and FTP** are valid *practical proxies* — not identical to the physiological thresholds (MLSS/critical power) they estimate.
- **Interval work:rest ratios** rest on a solid principle (recovery duration modulates aerobic vs anaerobic strain and time-at-intensity) but the exact numbers are conventions — the common worked examples all sit inside best practice (§9).
- **Interval type is specificity-driven** (§10): VO2 reps raise the ceiling, threshold/over-unders raise the sustainable floor, neuromuscular work improves economy. The mix should follow the *race's* intensity domain — sprint → severe/top-end; 70.3/IM → threshold + durability, the "fourth dimension" that long-course rewards.

## 1. Intensity distribution: ~80% easy is the real lever

**What's solid:** a large low-intensity base plus a meaningful dose of genuinely hard work beats a threshold-heavy ("grey zone") diet. Elite endurance athletes self-organise into ~75-80% of sessions below the aerobic threshold. Chronic moderate/threshold-dominant training is the distribution most consistently found *sub-optimal* for further gains in trained athletes.

**What's contested:** whether the hard ~20% should sit *above* threshold (polarised) or *at* threshold (pyramidal). The small RCTs lean polarised (Stöggl & Sperlich 2014: +11.7% VO2peak over 9 weeks; Esteve-Lanao 2007; Muñoz 2014), but a 2022 *MSSE* point/counterpoint left it formally unresolved — and reviews find most elite runners actually train **pyramidally**. Both beat threshold-heavy; the choice between them is phase- and athlete-dependent.

**Plan implication:**
- Anchor ~75-80% of training time at Z1-Z2. This is the least controversial, highest-value rule — and the one an athlete's pacing discipline most threatens (easy days drifting to tempo).
- Avoid the grey zone: don't let "easy" sessions creep to threshold.
- Treat polarised-vs-pyramidal as a phase choice: pyramidal/sweet-spot in base-build, shift toward polarised (sharper Z4-Z5) into the race-specific phase. For short, sharp events (e.g. a sprint triathlon), the race-specific phase leans polarised because the event rewards top-end.
- RCTs are short and small; effect sizes real but modest, and **adherence drives the difference** — which for most athletes means holding easy days easy is worth more than optimising the hard-day zone.

> Seiler & Kjerland (2006), *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 16(1):49-56 [verified]. Stöggl & Sperlich (2014), *Front Physiol* 5:33 [verified]. Burnley, Bearden & Jones (2022) vs Foster, Casado, Esteve-Lanao, Haugen & Seiler (2022), *MSSE* 54(6) — the point/counterpoint [verified]. Kenneally, Casado & Santos-Concejero (2018), *IJSPP* 13(9):1114-1121 [verified].

## 2. Periodisation: organise for peaking and overload management, not because it's proven superior

**Honest read:** the claim that a periodised plan beats an *equivalent non-periodised load* is weaker than commonly stated — most supporting meta-analyses (mostly resistance training) operationalised "non-periodised" as merely *unvaried* programmes and carried high risk of bias. Block periodisation shows a *small* edge over traditional in trained athletes (VO2max SMD ~0.40) but on low-quality evidence (mean PEDro 3.7/10).

**Plan implication:**
- Periodise for logistics, progression, peaking and avoiding monotony — not on a promise of a proven performance edge.
- **Base → Build → Peak → Taper** is a sound default *scaffold*; its value is organisational and peaking-oriented.
- Block periodisation (sequenced concentrated blocks) is a legitimate option for a time-crunched athlete who can't develop several capacities (e.g. swim/bike/run) simultaneously — relevant to anyone training on limited weekly hours.

> Issurin (2008), *J Sports Med Phys Fitness* 48(1):65-75 [verified]. Mølmen, Øfsteng & Rønnestad (2019), *Open Access J Sports Med* 10:145-160 [verified]. Afonso et al. (2019), *Front Physiol* 10:1023 [verified].

## 3. Taper: the numbers are real — trust them

**The strongest-evidenced topic here.** The canonical prescription from Bosquet's meta-analysis:
- **Duration:** ~2 weeks is most efficient (shorter, ~7-10 days, for shorter races / lower-volume athletes).
- **Volume:** reduce **41-60%**, progressively/exponentially.
- **Intensity:** **maintain.** This is the variable athletes most often get wrong by "resting up" — cutting intensity produces markedly smaller gains.
- **Frequency:** maintain (only slight reduction).
- **Expected gain:** overall ES 0.59 → roughly **2-3% performance improvement**. Meaningful (minutes over a middle-distance tri), not transformative — set expectations accordingly.

**Plan implication:** taper weeks should cut volume ~40-60% while holding the intensity touches (openers, race-pace surges). Watch pacing discipline here too — taper is not the week to "feel good and add a session."

> Bosquet et al. (2007), *MSSE* 39(8):1358-1365 [verified]. Mujika & Padilla (2003), *MSSE* 35(7):1182-1187 [verified]. Corroborated by a 2023 *PLOS One* meta-analysis (18(5):e0282838) [verified].

## 4. Supercompensation & 3:1 loading: useful heuristics, not proven ratios

**Honest read:** supercompensation comes from 1950s animal glycogen-overshoot work; there's little direct evidence its *time-course* is required for training adaptation. The **3:1** (3 load : 1 recovery) mesocycle is a coaching *convention* — no RCTs establish it as superior to 2:1, 4:1, or undulating patterns.

**Plan implication:**
- Cycle load and recovery — the *principle* of alternating stress and recovery is sound and universal.
- Hold the **3:1 ratio loosely.** Drop to 2:1 for a fatigued athlete, a masters athlete, or one returning from a break. For an older athlete with a demanding job, 2:1 is often the right default.
- Don't oversell "supercompensation" as hard science; frame recovery weeks as fatigue management, which is what's actually observable.

> Origin (Yakovlev, glycogen overshoot) and the 3:1 ratio: [unverified — heuristic, no controlled comparison of loading ratios located]. The critique that supercompensation is weakly evidenced is a credible, documented position.

## 5. The "10% rule": not a scientific law

**Honest read:** the 10%-per-week volume-increase cap is **expert opinion that became folklore** — no empirical validation. An RCT (Buist 2008) found no injury-rate difference between a ~10%-style graded progression and a steeper one; the threshold that *does* seem to matter is large spikes (roughly >30% jumps over two weeks), well above 10%.

**Plan implication:**
- Keep conservative progression as a sensible default — especially for **running** (the most injury-prone discipline) and return-from-injury. The instinct is fine.
- But judge by **response** (soreness, HR/pace decoupling, sleep, subjective) rather than the arithmetic. Rigid 10% capping can under-load a time-limited athlete.
- The real red flag is a sudden large spike, not exceeding a fixed percentage. (Note: this refines the CLAUDE.md "never increase weekly volume by more than 10%" line — keep it as a default guardrail, but the harder rule is "avoid big spikes; judge by response.")

> Nielsen RO et al. (2014) [verified]. Buist I et al. (2008), *Am J Sports Med* 36(1):33-39 [verified].

## 6. ACWR: a discredited number — don't use the threshold

**Honest read:** the Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio "sweet spot / danger zone" (Gabbett 2016) drove a decade of practice, then was substantially demolished on methodological grounds — mathematical coupling (acute load sits in both numerator and denominator), arbitrary windows, no predictive value beyond acute load alone. The "sweet spot" has been reproduced from *random data*.

**Plan implication:**
- Monitoring *rate of change* in load is a reasonable qualitative instinct — avoid sudden large jumps.
- **Do not use ACWR as a quantitative injury-prediction tool or a hard "1.5" threshold.** Prefer simpler defensible signals: sensible progression, adequate chronic base before hard blocks, and subjective/physiological monitoring (RPE, sleep, HRV, decoupling).

> Gabbett (2016), *Br J Sports Med* 50(5):273-280 [verified]. Impellizzeri et al. (2020), *IJSPP* [verified]. Lolli et al. (2017/2019) [verified].

## 7. CSS (Critical Swim Speed): valid proxy, re-test it

**What it is:** the swimming analogue of critical power — a threshold-ish velocity, close to (but not exactly) maximal lactate steady state. Origin: Wakayoshi et al. (1992), correlating strongly with OBLA velocity (r=0.949) and 400 m speed.

**The practical protocol:** time an all-out **400 m** and **200 m**; `CSS (m/s) = (400 − 200) / (t400 − t200)`. Set swim pace bands as s/100m offsets around it.

**Plan implication:**
- Use the 400/200 TT to set the athlete's personalised CSS, then prescribe swim zones as explicit /100m bands around it (always numbers, never adjectives like "easy" or "hard").
- Re-test every ~6-8 weeks / phase change; CSS shifts with fitness (the plan already schedules CSS retests).
- Treat CSS as a *training anchor*, not a physiological absolute. In open water, defer to HR/effort (see `signal-hierarchy.md` §3).

> Wakayoshi et al. (1992), *Eur J Appl Physiol Occup Physiol* 64(2):153-157 [verified]. Two-point 400/200 formula is the accepted field simplification of the same construct.

## 8. FTP / Critical Power: useful for zones, not identical to threshold

**What it is:** the highest power sustainable in quasi-steady state (~1 h). The "FTP = 0.95 × 20-min power" is a **protocol, not a physiological definition** (Allen & Coggan).

**The limitation:** FTP correlates highly with critical power (r≈0.97) but the limits of agreement are wide — they "should not be used interchangeably." CP itself sits ~20 W above MLSS, and the FTP-to-threshold offset is individual. Don't equate FTP with MLSS.

**Plan implication:**
- FTP (20-min test, or ramp/8-min variants) is fine for **setting bike zones and tracking change** — that's what it's good for. Schedule an FTP retest to anchor the power-based work in a build.
- Don't reason as if FTP = MLSS, and don't assume two athletes' FTPs mean the same "true threshold."
- Re-test each phase.

> Allen & Coggan (2006/2010), *Training and Racing with a Power Meter* [verified as method origin]. Karsten et al. (2020), *Front Physiol* 11:613151 [verified].

## 9. Interval work:rest ratios: grounded in principle, conventional in the exact numbers

**What's solid (the principle):** the recovery ("relief") interval — its duration *and* intensity — is a genuine, manipulable HIIT variable, not an afterthought. Buchheit & Laursen's framework treats it as one of the levers that shifts a session's balance between cardiopulmonary (aerobic), anaerobic and neuromuscular strain: change the recovery and you change what the session trains, even at the same work intensity.

**What the recovery controls:**
- **Shorter / incomplete recovery** keeps HR and VO2 elevated between reps → you re-reach the target faster and accumulate more *time at intensity* (the currency of VO2max and threshold adaptation). Trade-off: lower absolute power/pace on later reps.
- **Fuller recovery** restores the power/pace and anaerobic/neuromuscular quality of each rep, at the cost of less cumulative time at intensity.

**By session type (with evidence strength):**

| Session (rep length, intensity) | Work:rest | Rationale | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| VO2max (~3 min, Z5 / 115-120% FTP) | ~1:1 | Keep VO2 near-max between reps → bank time at VO2max | Classic VO2 ratio; well-grounded principle |
| Threshold (~4-5 min, Z4 / ~FTP) | ~2:1 to 5:3 | Sub-max effort; recovery = metabolite clearance + brief reset, not full | Seiler 4×4 used ~2 min recovery; Daniels cruise ~5:1 |
| Tempo (~10 min, Z3) | ~3:1 | Long reps bank time at tempo; brief float to reset | Daniels cruise-interval family |
| CSS swim (100m at CSS) | short (15-30 s) | Emulate continuous threshold; preserve the aerobic stimulus | Standard CSS practice |

**Worked examples — all within evidence-based best practice:**
- A **threshold run (4×4 min @ Z4, 2 min jog = 2:1)** uses the exact recovery structure of Seiler's much-studied 4×4/4×8/4×16 comparison — the most-validated interval-duration format (all three improved VO2peak; 4×8 was best at +11.4%, a hint that *threshold* reps can trend longer for more aerobic return).
- **VO2 work (5×3 min, 3 min recovery = 1:1)** is the textbook VO2max ratio.
- **FTP intervals (4×5 min, 3 min = 5:3)** and **tempo (2×10 min, 3 min ≈ 3:1)** sit sensibly in the threshold/tempo band — if anything slightly *generous* on recovery (safe, not aggressive).

**What's honest to flag:**
- **The exact numbers are evidence-informed conventions, not RCT-proven optima.** No trial establishes 4:2 as superior to 4:3 for threshold running; the *ranges* are sound, the precise figures sit in a defensible band. (Same epistemic status as 3:1 loading, §4.)
- **"Less recovery = more time at VO2max" is not unconditional.** For *short* reps (<~1-2 min), cutting recovery can drop power below the VO2max stimulus — a 2024 running study found intensified short intervals banked *less* time >90% VO2max than traditional long intervals. Traditional ~3-min VO2 reps at 1:1 sit on the well-supported side of that debate.

> Buchheit M, Laursen PB (2013). *Sports Med* 43(5):313-338 (Part I) & 43(10):927-954 (Part II) [verified] — HIIT-variable framework incl. relief duration/intensity. Seiler S, Jøranson K, Olesen BV, Hetlelid KJ (2013). *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 23(1):74-83 [verified] — 4×4 vs 4×8 vs 4×16 (max tolerable, ~2 min recovery), 4×8 best. Daniels J, *Daniels' Running Formula* — cruise/threshold intervals, ~1 min recovery per 5 min T-pace (~5:1) [coaching convention]. "Faster intervals, faster recoveries…", *Front Sports Act Living* (2024) [verified — short vs long VO2 intervals & time >90% VO2max]. See also `signal-hierarchy.md` §1 (anchor intervals to pace/power, not lagging HR).

## 10. Interval type → adaptation, and how it shifts with race distance

§9 covered *work:rest*. This covers *which interval, for which adaptation, for which race* — the specificity logic. It's the piece that changes most when moving from a short event (e.g. a sprint triathlon) to a long one (e.g. a 70.3 half-Ironman).

### 10.1 Interval type → primary adaptation

| Interval type | Rep length / intensity | Primary adaptation | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **VO2max** | 3–5 min @ ~vVO2max / 106–120% FTP | ↑ VO2max, max cardiac output / stroke volume, O2 delivery | Minutes accumulated ≥90% VO2max drive central (cardiac) remodelling + peripheral O2 extraction |
| **Threshold / sweet-spot / cruise** | 5–20 min @ ~FTP/CSS (Z3–Z4) | ↑ lactate threshold / FTP / **critical power**, lactate-clearance capacity, fractional utilization | Mitochondrial density + MCT lactate-transporter upregulation shift the metabolic steady-state boundary up |
| **Anaerobic capacity / speed-endurance** | 30 s–2 min, supra-threshold | ↑ glycolytic capacity, H⁺/lactate buffering & tolerance (raises VLaMax) | Up-regulated glycolytic flux + buffering; tolerance of high [La⁻]/low pH |
| **Neuromuscular / economy** | 6–15 s near-max, full recovery (strides, hill sprints) | ↑ running economy, neuromuscular power, tendon stiffness | Trains recruitment/firing + elastic energy return *without* glycolytic fatigue → each stride costs less O2 |

**The VLaMax caveat — flag it precisely.** Deliberately driving anaerobic/glycolytic capacity **up** can be *counterproductive for long-course* racing: a higher maximal glycolytic rate raises carbohydrate cost and suppresses fat oxidation at any given sub-max intensity (the "crossover" concept), and pushes the sustainable threshold *down*. The mechanism is solid; the specific "VLaMax" framing is largely practitioner/modelling (Mader's model, INSCYD) — there is **no trial showing "lower VLaMax → faster Ironman."** Use it as a *reason not to over-emphasise max-anaerobic work in a long-course build*, not as a hard number.

> Helgerud et al. (2007), *MSSE* 39(4):665–671 [verified] — 4×4 raised VO2max ~7–9% > moderate. Buchheit & Laursen (2013) Part I [verified]. Midgley, McNaughton & Wilkinson (2006), *Sports Med* 36(2):117–132 [verified] — time-at-VO2max. Poole, Burnley, Vanhatalo, Rossiter & Jones (2016), *MSSE* 48(11):2320–2334 [verified] — critical power. Barnes & Kilding (2015), *Sports Med-Open* 1(1):8; Denadai et al. (2017), *Sports Med* 47(3):545–554; Blagrove, Howatson & Hayes (2018), *Sports Med* 48(5):1117–1149 [all verified] — strength/plyometric → economy. Mader (2003), *Eur J Appl Physiol* 88(4–5):317–338 [verified — a model]; Brooks & Mercier (1994), *J Appl Physiol* 76(6):2253–2261 [verified — crossover]. *VLaMax-for-long-course: practitioner/modelling, no direct performance trial.*

### 10.2 Specificity: match the phase to the race's intensity domain

Physiology sorts effort into **intensity domains** — *moderate* (below LT1), *heavy* (LT1→threshold), *severe* (above threshold / critical power, where VO2max is reached before failure), *extreme*. **Critical power (CP) is the heavy/severe boundary** — the single most important fatigue threshold: below it, quasi-sustainable; above it, you spend a finite reserve (W′) and failure is a matter of time. The race-specific phase should bias toward the domain the race is contested in:

| Race | ~Duration | Contested in | Interval emphasis (specific phase) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sprint tri** | ~40 min | **Severe** — repeated surges above CP (starts, turns, run-off-bike) | VO2max (3–5 min) + anaerobic/W′ top-end + threshold to lift the ceiling |
| **Olympic** | ~2–2.5 h | **Heavy** — threshold-dominant | CP/threshold + sweet-spot; less pure VO2/anaerobic |
| **70.3 / IM** | ~4.5–6 h+ | **Heavy→moderate, sub-threshold for hours** | Sub-threshold/threshold sustainability, **over-unders**, long tempo, **durability + fuelling**; VO2 kept as *maintenance* |

This is why a **sprint build correctly leans severe/top-end** — and why a **70.3/IM build changes the whole diet**, not just the volume (§10.3).

> Burnley & Jones (2007), *Eur J Sport Sci* 7(2):63–79 [verified] — intensity domains. Burnley & Jones (2018), *Eur J Sport Sci* 18(1):1–12 [verified]. Poole et al. (2016) [verified]. Jones, Vanhatalo, Burnley, Morton & Poole (2010), *MSSE* 42(10):1876–1890 [verified]. Both races stay inside the ~80/20 frame (Seiler & Kjerland 2006; Seiler 2010 [verified]) — it's *which* 20% and *where*, not abandoning easy volume.

### 10.3 Durability — the "fourth dimension" (what long-course actually rewards)

For events lasting hours, the classic "big three" (VO2max, threshold, economy) explain ~72–88% of performance; the residual is increasingly attributed to **durability** — *the onset and magnitude of deterioration in the physiological profile (economy, threshold/CP, HR-at-power, substrate use) over prolonged exercise.* Formalised as a fourth profiling parameter (Maunder 2021) and elevated to "physiological resilience — the fourth dimension" (Jones 2024). It's real and measurable: **CP and W′ decline progressively** over 40/80/120 min of prolonged work.

**How you train it — the long-course toolkit:**
- **Prolonged aerobic volume** — long rides/runs that reach the fatigued state where drift appears.
- **Intervals performed pre-fatigued** — put the threshold/quality work *late* in a long session (threshold or over-under blocks in the final hour of a long ride) so you train *sustaining the profile under fatigue*, not fresh.
- **Bricks (bike→run)** — the triathlon-specific durability tool; economy and pace-holding on pre-fatigued legs. Disproportionately important for a 70.3, where a half-marathon is run off 90 km of bike.
- **Fuelling as a durability lever** — CP/late-race power is sensitive to carbohydrate availability; CHO ingestion **attenuates** the prolonged-exercise decline. Durability is both *trained* and *defended by practising race fuelling on long sessions*.

Nuance: durability improves with **both** low- and high-intensity training — not purely a "junk miles" adaptation — but the *dose of prolonged work* is what exposes and trains the fatigued-state profile.

> Maunder, Seiler, Mildenhall, Kilding & Plews (2021), *Sports Med* 51:1619–1628 [verified] — defines durability. Jones (2024), *J Physiol* 602(17) [verified] — "the fourth dimension." Clark et al. (2019), *J Appl Physiol* 127(3):726–736 [verified] — CP/W′ decline over time; CHO attenuates it. Matomäki et al. (2023), *Front Physiol* 14:1128111 [verified] — durability improves with LIT *and* HIT.

### 10.4 Other modalities — over-unders, progressions, fartlek

| Modality | What it develops | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Over-unders** (e.g., 2 min @ ~105% / 2 min @ ~90% threshold) | Clearing/re-metabolising lactate *while still working*; tolerating surges around threshold (hills, pace changes) | **Mechanism strong** (lactate shuttle, MCT upregulation); **format not RCT-proven** vs steady threshold — plausible + grounded, not "proven superior" |
| **Progressions / variable-intensity / fast-start intervals** | Pacing skill (negative-split), fast-twitch recruitment under fatigue, and **more time at VO2max** per session | **Moderate–strong** for variable/fast-start → time at VO2max (multiple controlled studies); **weaker** for "progression runs" as a named format |
| **Fartlek** ("speed play") | Flexible mixed-intensity, race-surge practice, by-feel/RPE training | **Low / practice-based** — no RCTs on fartlek itself; benefits inferred from variable-intensity literature. Pragmatic, athlete-friendly, not evidence-backed as a discrete method |

**Applying this:**
- **Over-unders** are a strong add for most endurance athletes — *especially* long-course (surge tolerance on undulating bike courses); race-relevant and threshold-building.
- **Fast-start / variable VO2 reps** are a legitimate way to bank more time at VO2max on turbo bike days if you want more stimulus per session (per §9).
- **Fartlek suits athletes with well-calibrated by-feel pacing** — for a strong RPE-pacer, unstructured speed-play is a low-friction, effective format, especially for run-surge practice. Just don't mistake it for a precision stimulus.

> Brooks (2018), *Cell Metab* 27(4):757–785 [verified — lactate shuttle]; Evertsen et al. (2001), *Acta Physiol Scand* [verified — MCT/lactate-threshold adaptation]. Bossi, Mesquida, Passfield, Rønnestad & Hopker (2020), *IJSPP* 15(7):982–989 [verified] — varied-power intervals 410 vs 286 s >90% VO2max. Bailey, Vanhatalo, DiMenna, Wilkerson & Jones (2011), *MSSE* 43(3):457–467 [verified] — fast-start speeds VO2 kinetics. Rønnestad et al. (2015), *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 25(2):143–151 & (2020) 30(5):849–857 [verified] — short intervals > effort-matched long. Fartlek origin: Gösta Holmér, Sweden, late 1930s [coaching history].

## One-line summary for prescribing

*Taper hard on the numbers. Keep ~80% of training easy. Decide polarised-vs-pyramidal by phase, don't dogmatise. Treat 3:1 loading, the 10% rule, ACWR thresholds, and "FTP = threshold" as convenient rules of thumb — use them, but never quote them as settled science.*
