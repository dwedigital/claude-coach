## Your Specific Technique Fixes

Based on your January 2026 swim coaching notes, here are your issues mapped to resources:

### 1. Head Position Too High on Inhale

**The problem:** Lifting your head to breathe raises your hips and legs, creating drag. You should rotate to breathe, not lift.

**The fix:** Keep one goggle in the water when you breathe. Your head should rotate with your body, not independently. Think about breathing into the pocket of air created by your bow wave.

**Watch:**
- [GTN — How to Breathe on Both Sides While Swimming](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/gtn-presents-how-to-breathe-on-both-sides-while-swimming/) — covers head position during breathing
- [Effortless Swimming — Freestyle Technique: What They Didn't Teach You in Swim School](https://effortlessswimming.com/freestyle-technique-what-they-didnt-teach-you-in-swim-school/) — covers head position fundamentals

**Drills to practise:**
- **Side kick drill** — kick on your side with bottom arm extended, top arm resting on your hip. Head neutral, breathe by rotating slightly. Builds comfort with low head position
- **6-3-6 drill** — 6 kicks on your side, 3 strokes, 6 kicks on the other side. Forces you to rotate for breathing rather than lifting

### 2. Legs Low in Water / Kicking Too Fast

**The problem:** A fast, knee-driven kick wastes energy and doesn't propel you. Low legs create massive drag. This is often caused by head position (see above) and poor core engagement.

**The fix:** Kick from the hips, not the knees. Smaller, faster flutter kick. Think about your legs being within the "shadow" of your body — they shouldn't be hanging below you. Fix your head position and your legs will often follow.

**Watch:**
- [Effortless Swimming — The 5 Core Principles of Fast Freestyle](https://effortlessswimming.com/5-core-principles-of-fast-freestyle/) — covers body position and kick
- Search YouTube: "Effortless Swimming kick technique" or "GTN freestyle kick triathlon"

**Drills to practise:**
- **Kick with a board** — hold a kickboard, face down, kick from hips. Keep kick small and fast. If your knees are bending a lot, you're doing it wrong
- **Vertical kicking** — tread water using only flutter kick in deep end. Arms crossed on chest. 30 seconds on, 30 seconds rest. Brutal but effective
- **Streamline kick on back** — arms overhead, kick on your back. If your knees break the surface, your kick is too big

### 3. Recovery Arm: Elbow Not Relaxed

**The problem:** A stiff, straight-arm recovery swings wide and disrupts rotation. It also wastes energy.

**The fix:** Lead the recovery with your elbow, not your hand. Your arm should be relaxed with a high elbow — think about pulling your hand out of your back pocket and swinging your elbow forward with your hand dangling.

**Watch:**
- [GTN — 6 Swim Drills to Improve Freestyle Swimming Technique](https://www.globaltrinetwork.com/video/6-swim-drills-to-improve-freestyle-swimming-technique-what-does-swimming-drill-mean) — includes fingertip drag drill
- [Triathlete.com — Fingertip Drag Drill](https://www.triathlete.com/training/triathlon-swim-drill-fingertip-drag-drill/) — detailed breakdown

**Drills to practise:**
- **Fingertip drag** — swim freestyle, but drag your fingertips along the water surface during the recovery phase. Forces a high elbow. This is in your training plan already
- **Zipper drill** — similar to fingertip drag, but run your thumb up your side (like a zipper) from hip to armpit before extending forward

### 4. Underwater Pull Too Straight / Needs High Elbow Catch

**The problem:** A straight-arm pull under water is like paddling a canoe — inefficient. You want to "anchor" your hand and forearm in the water and pull your body past it.

**The fix:** Early vertical forearm (EVF). As your hand enters the water, tip your fingertips down while keeping your elbow high. Think about reaching over a barrel. Pull with your forearm AND hand, not just your hand.

**Watch:**
- [Effortless Swimming — The Four Key Positions for an Epic Catch and Pull](https://effortlessswimming.com/the-four-key-position-for-an-epic-catch-and-pull/) — the best explanation of the catch
- [Effortless Swimming — THIS Is Why It's So Hard to Get a High Elbow Catch](https://effortlessswimming.com/hard-get-high-elbow-catch/) — common mistakes
- [Effortless Swimming — Two Drills for High Elbow Freestyle](https://effortlessswimming.com/two-drills-for-high-elbow-freestyle/)
- [GTN — 3 Swim Drills to Improve Your Freestyle Catch](https://www.globaltrinetwork.com/video/3-swim-drills-to-improve-your-freestyle-catch-front-crawl-swimming-technique)

**Drills to practise:**
- **Fist drill** — swim with closed fists. Forces you to catch with your forearm, not just your palm. When you open your hands afterwards, the catch feels completely different. This is in your training plan
- **Catch-up drill** — one arm stays extended while the other completes a full stroke cycle. Slows everything down so you can focus on the catch phase. In your plan
- **Scull drill** — float face-down with arms extended, sweep your hands in small figure-8 patterns. Teaches you to feel the water pressure on your forearms

### 5. Pulling on Leading Arm Too Early

**The problem:** Starting your pull before your recovering arm has entered the water creates a "windmill" effect. You lose your glide and distance per stroke drops.

**The fix:** Be patient with the leading arm. Keep it extended until the other hand enters the water near it (or at least until the other arm passes your head). The catch-up drill is specifically designed for this.

**Drills to practise:**
- **Catch-up drill** — don't start pulling until your recovering hand touches your extended hand. Exaggerates the pause but teaches patience
- **3/4 catch-up** — slightly less exaggerated: start pulling when your recovering arm passes your head (not full catch-up, but still delayed). More race-realistic

### 6. Breathing Pattern — Switch to Every 3 Strokes

**The problem:** Breathing every 4 strokes means you always breathe to the same side. In open water, you need bilateral breathing for sighting, navigation, and handling waves/swimmers on either side.

**The fix:** Switch to breathing every 3 strokes (bilateral). It will feel harder initially because you get less air. Start by doing 50m sets alternating: 25m breathe every 3, 25m breathe every 2 (recovery). Build up.

**Watch:**
- [GTN — How to Breathe on Both Sides While Swimming](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/gtn-presents-how-to-breathe-on-both-sides-while-swimming/)
- [220 Triathlon — Improve Your Swim Breathing](https://www.220triathlon.com/training/swim-training/improve-your-swim-breathing-in-triathlon-training)

**Drills to practise:**
- **Bilateral breathing sets** — 4x100m breathing every 3 strokes. Focus on exhaling fully underwater so you're ready to inhale quickly when you rotate
- **Mixed breathing** — breathe every 3 for the main set, every 2 for warm-up and cool-down. Gradually increase the proportion done on 3

## Open Water Specific

You need at least 3-4 open water sessions before race day. Pool ≠ sea.

### Sighting
- **Watch:** [GTN — Top Tips to Master Open Water Triathlon Swimming](https://www.globaltrinetwork.com/video/top-tips-to-master-open-water-triathlon-swimming) — covers sighting from 4:45
- **Watch:** [Triathlete.com — How to Sight in Open Water: 9 Techniques](https://www.triathlete.com/training/9-secrets-to-proper-open-water-sighting/)
- **Key technique:** Sight-and-breathe: lift eyes just above the waterline (crocodile eyes), spot your target, then rotate to breathe. Every 6-10 strokes. Don't lift your whole head
- **Practise in the pool:** Add sighting to your warm-up — every 6 strokes, lift your eyes to look at the clock or a landmark at the end of the pool. Get used to the movement without breaking stroke

### Drafting
- Swim on someone's feet (3% energy saving) or on their hip (7% energy saving)
- In a race, find someone slightly faster than you and sit on their feet
- Practise with a training partner if possible

### Dealing with Anxiety
- [Mottiv — How to Swim Panic-Free in Open Water](https://www.mymottiv.com/blog/race-prep-fear-of-open-water)
- First open water session: just float, breathe, get comfortable. Don't try to swim hard
- Wetsuit changes buoyancy — practise in it before race day

## Session Structure Guide

If you're ever swimming on your own and want to structure a session:

**Watch:** [GTN — Structuring a Swim Session: The Ultimate Beginners Guide](https://www.globaltrinetwork.com/video/structuring-a-swim-session-the-ultimate-beginners-guide)

### Template (1,000m session, ~30 min)
```
Warm-up:     200m easy freestyle
Drills:      4x50m (pick 2 drills, alternate)
Main set:    5x100m at steady pace (20-30s rest)
Cool-down:   100m easy
```

### Template (1,500m session, ~45 min)
```
Warm-up:     200m easy freestyle
Drills:      4x50m technique focus
Main set:    Option A: 1,000m continuous (race rehearsal)
             Option B: 5x200m at race pace (30s rest)
Pull:        200m with pull buoy (isolate upper body)
Cool-down:   100m easy
```

## Equipment Worth Considering

- **Pull buoy** — isolates your upper body, teaches you what good body position feels like. Use in warm-up/cool-down
- **Swim snorkel (centre-mount)** — lets you focus on stroke without worrying about breathing. Great for drill work
- **Fins** — short fins help with body position and kick technique. Don't rely on them but they're useful for drill work
- **Wetsuit** — you'll need one for the race. Get it early and practise in it at least 3 times in open water before June 28
