# Situational Protocols — Illness, Heat, Travel, Detraining, Disruption

**Purpose:** how to adjust training around the disruptions a plan can't predict. Read this when the athlete reports illness, a hot race, travel, a forced break, or missed sessions.

**Scope & safety.** This is coaching decision-support, **not medical diagnosis**. Two sections — §1 (illness/myocarditis) and §3 (RED-S) — are **hard safety gates**, not tunable coaching parameters: they trigger a "stop and see a doctor" response, full stop. Everything else is coach-tunable.

---

## 1. Return to training after illness — ⚠️ SAFETY GATE

**The one non-negotiable rule: fever or systemic illness = do not train. No exceptions, no "I feel okay-ish."**

**The neck check (a conservative heuristic, not a validated test):**
- **Above the neck only** (runny nose, sneezing, mild sore throat) *and* no fever, no systemic signs → a gentle, reduced session is generally acceptable; reassess as you go.
- **Below the neck / systemic** (fever, chills, body aches, productive cough, chest tightness, GI upset, swollen glands, marked malaise) → **rest completely** until resolved.

**Graded return after a systemic illness:** resume easy/aerobic only, low volume, rebuild conservatively (2:1 loading) over days-to-weeks by severity. Do not reintroduce intensity while any systemic symptom, resting-HR elevation, or unusual breathlessness/fatigue persists.

**Why fever is a hard stop — myocarditis.** Cardiotropic viruses can inflame the heart muscle during a systemic infection. Exercising through that window worsens outcomes (increased viral/organism damage + amplified immune attack on heart tissue) and myocarditis is a recognised cause of sudden cardiac death in young, fit people — and it can be clinically silent early. The neck check was never designed to detect cardiac risk; it must never be used to justify training through a fever.

**🚑 See a doctor before resuming if:** any persistent post-illness chest pain, palpitations, breathlessness out of proportion to effort, or exercise intolerance. These are medical, not coaching, matters. The coach's job here is to hold the line, not to clear the athlete.

> Friman & Ilbäck (1998), *Int J Sports Med* 19(Suppl 3):S172-S182 [verified]. Friman, Larsson & Rolf (1997), *Scand J Infect Dis Suppl* 104:41-49 [verified]. Weidner & Schurr (2003), *Br J Sports Med* 37(4):304-306 [verified] — afebrile URTI: moderate exercise didn't worsen a head cold (supports the "above the neck" half). Neck-check origin: Eichner (heuristic).

## 2. Overtraining vs overreaching

A continuum, distinguished only in retrospect by how long recovery takes:
- **Functional overreaching (FOR):** intended. Short-term performance dip + fatigue from a hard block; **supercompensates** above baseline after days-to-~2 weeks recovery. This is the goal of a load block.
- **Non-functional overreaching (NFOR):** load/recovery balance broken. Performance stagnates/declines, recovery takes weeks-to-months, no supercompensation. Cost paid without return.
- **Overtraining syndrome (OTS):** maladaptive end; recovery takes months+. A **diagnosis of exclusion**.

**There is no single validated diagnostic marker.** Monitor *trends*: unexplained performance decline at a given effort, persistent fatigue, disturbed mood/sleep, rising perceived effort at fixed workloads, elevated (or in OTS, blunted) HR responses. The best preventive lever is planned recovery (the 3:1/2:1 mesocycle) and trend monitoring — not any one biomarker.

**🚑 Safety flag:** persistent unexplained fatigue/performance decline warrants a **medical check** (rule out anaemia, thyroid, post-viral, RED-S) rather than "push through." Coaching can manage load; it can't diagnose the cause of chronic fatigue.

> Meeusen et al. (2013), ECSS/ACSM joint consensus, *MSSE* 45(1):186-205 [verified].

## 3. RED-S / low energy availability — ⚠️ SAFETY GATE

**Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport** = impaired physiological function from a mismatch between energy intake and the cost of training + living. **Not just a female or elite issue** — an amateur who ramps a big block while under-eating (dieting, or simply not fuelling the extra load) is squarely at risk.

**Warning signs to surface:** unexplained performance plateau/decline, recurrent fatigue, poor recovery; frequent illness; recurrent or non-healing injuries, **stress fractures / low bone health**; reduced libido, low mood, feeling cold, unintended weight loss, disordered-eating patterns.

**Coaching action: fuel the training, don't add more training — and refer.** A big block on inadequate fuel produces the *opposite* of the intended adaptation.

**🚑 Safety flag:** RED-S, disordered eating, and stress fractures are **clinical conditions**. Flag the signs and advise fuelling + referral to a sports physician / registered dietitian. Never design a deliberate energy deficit for a training athlete, and never attempt to manage disordered eating.

> Mountjoy et al. (2014), *Br J Sports Med* 48(7):491-497 [verified]; 2018 update 52(11):687-697 [verified]; 2023 REDs consensus 57(17):1073-1097 [verified].

## 4. Heat acclimatisation

**Timeline:** repeated exercise-heat exposures over **~10-14 days**, on consecutive days, ~60-90 min each. **Most of the benefit is banked in the first ~5-7 exposures**; the rest consolidates across the second week. For a hot race, start ≥2 weeks out, before tapering.

**Adaptations (why it works):** plasma-volume expansion (within days) → lower HR at a given workload; earlier, higher, more dilute sweating; lower core/skin temperature; reduced cardiovascular strain. The same effort feels easier and is safer.

**Note for reviews:** during acclimatisation, expect elevated HR early in the block (before plasma volume expands) — that's the adaptation in progress, not overreach. Cross-reference with `signal-hierarchy.md` §5 (heat is an HR confound).

**🚑 Safety flag:** heat illness (exhaustion, exertional heat stroke) is a **medical emergency**. Acclimatisation reduces risk but doesn't remove it. Confusion, collapse, or stopping sweating → stop and seek help.

> Périard, Racinais & Sawka (2015), *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 25(Suppl 1):20-38 [verified]. Racinais et al. (2015), heat consensus, same issue, 6-19 [verified].

## 5. Hydration in the heat — drink to thirst, don't force fluids

Two evidence streams, both real; present the reconciliation, not dogma:
- **Dehydration impairs performance** and raises thermal strain (classically the >2% body-mass-loss point in the heat). Start euhydrated; on hot/long sessions have a fluid + sodium plan.
- **But overdrinking is a genuine, occasionally fatal hazard** — exercise-associated hyponatraemia (EAH) from drinking beyond thirst. For most athletes, **drinking to thirst is sufficient.**

**Reconciliation for coaching:** start euhydrated; on hot/long efforts have a fluid + sodium plan and don't let big deficits accumulate; but **do not force fluids on a rigid schedule beyond thirst.** Practise race-day fluid/sodium strategy on the bike in training.

**🚑 Safety flag:** EAH is a medical emergency. Blanket "drink more" advice is actively unsafe. Confusion / severe headache / collapse after prolonged exercise → urgent care.

> Sawka et al. (2007), ACSM position stand, *MSSE* 39(2):377-390 [verified]. Hew-Butler et al. (2017), 3rd International EAH Consensus [verified]. Noakes / Hoffman — thirst-adequacy and the overdrinking hypothesis [verified].

## 6. Travel & jet lag

- **Travel fatigue** (tiredness of the journey itself) resolves with a day or two of rest, regardless of time zones.
- **Jet lag** (circadian misalignment) re-entrains at **~1 time zone per day.** Plan arrival before a key event accordingly.
- **Eastward is harder** than westward (phase advance vs the easier phase delay) — budget more days for eastward trips.
- **Light is the primary lever:** time bright-light exposure and avoidance toward destination time; shift sleep, meals, and training toward destination time on arrival. Melatonin and strategic caffeine/napping are adjuncts. Hydrate in-flight.

**Note:** performance has a circadian rhythm (peaks late afternoon/early evening) — a race in a new time zone before re-entrainment may fall at a physiological low. If the athlete's races are mostly local, this matters mainly for travel to away events.

> Samuels (2012), *Clin J Sport Med* 22(3):268-273 [verified]. Janse van Rensburg et al. (2021), *Sports Med* 51(10):2029-2050 [verified].

## 7. Detraining — a short break costs almost nothing

Split by layoff length:
- **Short (<~2-4 weeks — a taper, an illness week, a forced break):** VO2max drops modestly early (~4-6% over the first 2-3 weeks), driven largely by **plasma-volume loss** (which contracts within days but also rebounds fast). Oxidative enzymes decline. **Net message: a short break costs little real fitness — often negligible, and a taper is net-positive. Don't panic-train after a short illness layoff.**
- **Long (>~4 weeks):** VO2max keeps declining but in a trained athlete stays above untrained baseline; the **most recent gains are lost first.** Sustained losses in stroke volume, cardiac dimensions, enzymes, capillarisation.

**Plan implication:** reassure and rebuild, don't cram. After a short illness/travel break, resume near where the athlete left off (adjusting per §1 if illness); after a long break, rebuild conservatively and expect the freshest fitness to return quickest.

> Mujika & Padilla (2000), Detraining Part I *Sports Med* 30(2):79-87 & Part II 30(3):145-154 [verified].

## 8. Missed-session triage — judgment, not a formula

**No primary evidence base — this is coaching judgment.** Sensible heuristics:
- **Don't cram.** Two missed sessions don't become one double session. Respect the volume-progression ceiling and recovery logic.
- **Protect the key sessions, sacrifice the filler.** Keep the one or two sessions carrying the training intent (long ride, threshold set); drop easy volume, which is more replaceable.
- **Redistribute forward** across subsequent weeks rather than compressing into the current one.
- **Cause dictates the response:** missed for *illness* → §1 (don't backfill, rebuild conservatively); *life/travel* → gentle redistribution; *accumulating fatigue* → the miss may be protective, treat it as a signal (§2).
- **A single missed session is usually noise** (§7 — short interruptions are cheap). Don't over-correct.

Always update `plans/current-plan.md` to reflect what actually happened vs what was planned (per the hard rules in CLAUDE.md).

## Quick-reference: gate vs tunable

| Situation | Type | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Fever / systemic illness | 🚑 **Gate** | No training until resolved; post-viral cardiac symptoms → doctor |
| RED-S / disordered eating / stress fracture signs | 🚑 **Gate** | Fuel + refer; never prescribe a deficit |
| Heat illness / EAH symptoms | 🚑 **Gate** | Stop, seek help |
| Persistent unexplained fatigue/decline | 🚑 **Gate-ish** | Medical check to exclude causes |
| Heat acclimatisation | Tunable | ~10-14 day build, expect early HR elevation |
| Hydration | Tunable | Drink to thirst + sodium plan for long/hot |
| Jet lag | Tunable | ~1 zone/day, eastward harder, light-led |
| Detraining | Tunable | Short break ≈ free; reassure, don't cram |
| Missed sessions | Tunable | Protect key work, redistribute, don't cram |
